
# Conic Sections — NCERT Class 11 Mathematics, Chapter 10

*Level: Class 11 Board / JEE Foundation*

> How a flat cut through a cone gives you circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas — and how each one turns into a clean algebraic equation once you place it on coordinate axes.

**Prerequisites:** distance formula, section formula, straight lines (slope, intercepts), completing the square.

---

## 10.1 Introduction ⭐

A **conic section** (or *conic*) is any curve obtained by slicing a **double-napped right circular cone** with a plane. Apollonius (262–190 B.C.) named the parabola and the hyperbola; the circle and ellipse were known earlier. Conics show up whenever a distance-based constraint defines a curve — planetary orbits, telescope and antenna reflectors, headlight and flashlight reflectors, and (as the Miscellaneous Examples below show) suspension-bridge cables.

A right circular cone is generated by rotating a line \(m\) (the **generator**) about a fixed intersecting line \(l\) (the **axis**) while keeping the angle between them, \(\alpha\), constant. The intersection point \(V\) is the **vertex**, and \(V\) splits the cone into two **nappes** (upper and lower).

---

## 10.2 Sections of a Cone ⭐

Let \(\beta\) be the angle the cutting plane makes with the axis of the cone. Which curve you get depends entirely on how \(\beta\) compares with the cone's own half-angle \(\alpha\), and on whether the plane passes through the vertex.

### 10.2.1 Non-degenerate cases (plane misses the vertex)


| Condition on\(\beta\)         | Curve produced | Geometric picture                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| \(\beta = 90^\circ\)          | **Circle**     | plane perpendicular to the axis                      |
| \(\alpha < \beta < 90^\circ\) | **Ellipse**    | plane tilted, but still crosses only one nappe fully |
| \(\beta = \alpha\)            | **Parabola**   | plane parallel to exactly one generator              |
| \(0 \le \beta < \alpha\)      | **Hyperbola**  | plane steep enough to cut*both* nappes               |

In the first three rows the plane cuts all the way across a single nappe; in the last row it cuts through both nappes, producing the two separate branches of a hyperbola.

### 10.2.2 Degenerate conics (plane passes through the vertex)


| Condition on\(\beta\)           | Section                                             | Degenerate form of |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| \(\alpha < \beta \le 90^\circ\) | a single**point**                                   | (limiting case)    |
| \(\beta = \alpha\)              | a**straight line** (the plane contains a generator) | parabola           |
| \(0 \le \beta < \alpha\)        | a**pair of intersecting straight lines**            | hyperbola          |

> **Watch out:** "Degenerate" doesn't mean "wrong" — it means the constant that defines the curve (radius, sum/difference of focal distances) has shrunk to zero or the defining point sits on the defining line. It's the same family of equations at a boundary value, not a different topic.

The rest of this note derives the standard *algebraic* equation for each non-degenerate conic, by placing it in the most convenient position relative to the coordinate axes — vertex/centre at the origin, axis of symmetry along a coordinate axis.

---

## 10.3 Circle ⭐

**Definition.** A circle is the set of all points in a plane equidistant from one fixed point, the **centre**; that fixed distance is the **radius**.

### Standard equation — general case first

Take centre \(C(h,k)\) and radius \(r\). For any point \(P(x,y)\) on the circle, \(|CP| = r\). By the distance formula,
\[
\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2} = r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2 = r^2}
\]
Squaring is safe here with no extraneous-root risk, because both sides were already non-negative (a distance and a given positive radius).

Setting \(h=k=0\) recovers the simplest case, centre at the origin: \(x^2+y^2=r^2\). Notice the source already derives the *general* centre-\((h,k)\) case first and treats the origin-centred circle as the special case — exactly the derivation order this note follows throughout.

#### 10.3 Solved — Circle from an off-origin equation (NCERT Example 3)

**Given** \(x^2+y^2+8x+10y-8=0\).
**Approach** Group the \(x\)- and \(y\)-terms and complete the square in each.
\[
(x^2+8x+16)+(y^2+10y+25) = 8+16+25 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x+4)^2+(y+5)^2 = 49
\]
**Work/Check** So centre \((-4,-5)\), radius \(7\). (This is a specific instance of the general pattern in the Glossary's *general form* row — completing the square on \(x^2+y^2+2gx+2fy+c=0\) always gives centre \((-g,-f)\) and \(r=\sqrt{g^2+f^2-c}\); here \(g=4,f=5,c=-8\) gives \(r=\sqrt{16+25+8}=7\), confirming the answer.)

#### 10.3 Solved — Three conditions pin down a circle (NCERT Example 4)

**Given** circle through \((2,-2)\) and \((3,4)\), centre on the line \(x+y=2\).
**Approach** Use the general form \((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\) and turn each condition into an equation in \(h,k,r^2\).
**Work**
\[
(2-h)^2+(-2-k)^2=r^2,\qquad (3-h)^2+(4-k)^2=r^2,\qquad h+k=2
\]
Solving the three equations together gives \(h=0.7,\ k=1.3,\ r^2=12.58\).
**Check** \((2-0.7)^2+(-2-1.3)^2 = 1.69+10.89=12.58\) ✓, and \((3-0.7)^2+(4-1.3)^2=5.29+7.29=12.58\) ✓, and \(0.7+1.3=2\) ✓ — all three original conditions hold, so the answer is trustworthy, not just algebraically produced.
\[
\boxed{(x-0.7)^2+(y-1.3)^2=12.58}
\]

## 10.4 Parabola ⭐⭐

**Definition.** A parabola is the set of all points in a plane equidistant from a fixed line (the **directrix**) and a fixed point not on that line (the **focus**).

> **Watch out — the degenerate case:** if the "fixed point" were allowed to sit *on* the fixed line, the locus collapses to the straight line through that point, perpendicular to the directrix. That's why the definition explicitly excludes it.

The line through the focus perpendicular to the directrix is the **axis**; where the parabola crosses its axis is the **vertex**.

### 10.4.1 Standard equations — derive one orientation, then use symmetry for the rest

**Set-up.** Put the vertex at the origin, focus at \(F(a,0)\) with \(a>0\), directrix \(x=-a\). For \(P(x,y)\) on the parabola, the defining condition is \(PF = PB\), where \(B(-a,y)\) is the foot of the perpendicular from \(P\) to the directrix.
\[
\sqrt{(x-a)^2+y^2} = \sqrt{(x+a)^2}
\]
Squaring both sides (valid here because both sides are non-negative distances, so no extraneous root is introduced):
\[
(x-a)^2+y^2=(x+a)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2-2ax+a^2+y^2=x^2+2ax+a^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{y^2=4ax}
\]
The converse also holds — any \((x,y)\) satisfying \(y^2=4ax\) really does satisfy \(PF=PB\) (substitute back and simplify), so the equation captures the *whole* locus, not just a necessary condition.

**Finishing the other three orientations by reflection, not by fresh derivation.** Reflecting the whole plane in the \(y\)-axis (\(x \to -x\)) sends focus \((a,0)\to(-a,0)\) and directrix \(x=-a \to x=a\), and reflections preserve distances — so the *same* focus–directrix condition, now with the reflected focus/directrix, is satisfied exactly by \(y^2=-4ax\). That is the left-opening parabola; no new derivation is needed, only the symmetry argument. Swapping the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) (reflection in the line \(y=x\)) turns the whole picture 90°, so the same logic gives the up/down-opening parabolas \(x^2=4ay\) and \(x^2=-4ay\).


| Orientation | Equation     | Focus      | Directrix | Opens                                            |
| ------------- | -------------- | ------------ | ----------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Right       | \(y^2=4ax\)  | \((a,0)\)  | \(x=-a\)  | toward\(+x\) if coefficient of \(x\) is positive |
| Left        | \(y^2=-4ax\) | \((-a,0)\) | \(x=a\)   | toward\(-x\)                                     |
| Up          | \(x^2=4ay\)  | \((0,a)\)  | \(y=-a\)  | toward\(+y\)                                     |
| Down        | \(x^2=-4ay\) | \((0,-a)\) | \(y=a\)   | toward\(-y\)                                     |

In every row \(a>0\) is a distance; the equation's own sign (not \(a\)) tells you the direction. A quick pattern for reading any given equation: **which variable is squared fixes the axis of symmetry** (a \(y^2\) term ⟹ axis along \(x\); an \(x^2\) term ⟹ axis along \(y\)), and **the sign of the linear term's coefficient fixes the direction** (positive ⟹ opens toward \(+x\)/\(+y\); negative ⟹ opens toward \(-x\)/\(-y\)).

### 10.4.2 Latus rectum ⭐

**Definition.** The latus rectum is the chord through the focus, perpendicular to the axis, with both endpoints on the parabola.

For \(y^2=4ax\): the directrix-distance argument that built the curve also gives \(AF=2a\) for an endpoint \(A\) of the latus rectum (since \(AF\) equals the perpendicular distance from \(A\) to the directrix, which by construction of the figure is \(2a\)). By symmetry about the axis, the other endpoint \(B\) has \(FB=2a\) too, so
\[
\boxed{\text{length of latus rectum} = AB = 4a}
\]

#### 10.4 Solved — Reading off focus, directrix, latus rectum (NCERT Example 5)

**Given** \(y^2=8x\). **Approach** Match against \(y^2=4ax\): \(4a=8 \Rightarrow a=2\).
**Work/Check** Focus \((2,0)\); directrix \(x=-2\); latus rectum \(4a=8\). Coefficient of \(x\) is positive, so it opens right — consistent with the diagram above.

#### 10.4 Solved — Building the equation from focus + directrix (NCERT Example 6)

Focus \((2,0)\), directrix \(x=-2\) ⟹ axis is the \(x\)-axis, opening right, \(a=2\): \(\boxed{y^2=8x}\).

#### 10.4 Solved — Building the equation from vertex + focus (NCERT Example 7)

Vertex \((0,0)\), focus \((0,2)\) lies on the \(y\)-axis ⟹ form \(x^2=4ay\), \(a=2\): \(\boxed{x^2=8y}\).

#### 10.4 Solved — Using a point plus symmetry to fix the sign (NCERT Example 8)

Symmetric about the \(y\)-axis, through \((2,-3)\). Since \((2,-3)\) has negative \(y\), the parabola must open **downward**: form \(x^2=-4ay\).
\[
2^2=-4a(-3) \;\Longrightarrow\; 4=12a \;\Longrightarrow\; a=\tfrac13 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{3x^2=-4y}
\]
**Check:** at \(x=2\), \(y=-3x^2/4=-3\) ✓.

## 10.5 Ellipse ⭐⭐⭐

**Definition.** An ellipse is the set of all points in a plane whose distances from two fixed points (the **foci**) sum to a constant.

> **Watch out:** that constant sum must be *strictly greater* than the distance between the two foci, or no such point can exist (triangle inequality).

The midpoint of the foci is the **centre**; the line through the foci is the **major axis**; the line through the centre perpendicular to it is the **minor axis**; the major axis's endpoints are the **vertices**.

### 10.5.1 Relating semi-major axis \(a\), semi-minor axis \(b\), and focal distance \(c\)

Let the foci be a distance \(2c\) apart, and let the constant sum of distances be \(2a\) (so a vertex \(P\), at one end of the major axis, gives \(F_1P+F_2P = (c+a)+(a-c)=2a\), confirming the constant equals the major-axis length). Let \(Q\) be an endpoint of the minor axis, at perpendicular distance \(b\) from the centre; by the Pythagorean distance to each focus, \(F_1Q=F_2Q=\sqrt{b^2+c^2}\), so
\[
2\sqrt{b^2+c^2}=2a \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{a^2=b^2+c^2}\quad\text{i.e.}\quad c=\sqrt{a^2-b^2}
\]

### 10.5.2 Eccentricity

\[
\boxed{e=\dfrac{c}{a}}
\]
the ratio of centre-to-focus distance to centre-to-vertex distance. Since \(0<c<a\) for a genuine ellipse, \(0<e<1\). In terms of \(e\), each focus sits a distance \(ae\) from the centre.

### 10.5.3 Standard equations — derive one orientation, then use symmetry

**Set-up.** Foci \(F_1(-c,0)\), \(F_2(c,0)\); constant sum \(2a\). For \(P(x,y)\) on the ellipse, \(PF_1+PF_2=2a\). Using the distance formula and isolating one radical before squaring (twice, to clear both radicals in turn — each squaring step is legitimate here because the intermediate quantity being squared, \(2a-PF_2\), is non-negative for any point actually on the ellipse):
\[
\sqrt{(x+c)^2+y^2} = 2a-\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2}
\]
Squaring, simplifying, and squaring again eventually collapses to
\[
\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{a^2-c^2}=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1}\quad (b^2=a^2-c^2)
\]
and the converse direction (any point satisfying this equation really does have \(PF_1+PF_2=2a\)) confirms the equation is the full locus, with no spurious points introduced by the two squarings.

**Finishing the other orientation.** Swapping the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) (reflection in \(y=x\)) turns foci-on-\(x\)-axis into foci-on-\(y\)-axis without any new distance-formula work: \(\dfrac{x^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{a^2}=1\), where now the *larger* denominator sits under \(y^2\).

### 10.5.4 Latus rectum

Same idea as the parabola: for an endpoint \(A=(c,\ell)\) of the latus rectum through \(F_2(c,0)\), substitute into the ellipse equation:
\[
\frac{(ae)^2}{a^2}+\frac{\ell^2}{b^2}=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \ell^2=b^2(1-e^2)=b^2\left(1-\frac{a^2-b^2}{a^2}\right)=\frac{b^4}{a^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \ell=\frac{b^2}{a}
\]
By symmetry, the full chord length (latus rectum) is
\[
\boxed{\frac{2b^2}{a}}
\]

#### 10.5 Solved — Reading every parameter off a standard equation (NCERT Example 9)

**Given** \(\dfrac{x^2}{25}+\dfrac{y^2}{9}=1\). **Approach** Denominator under \(x^2\) is larger (\(25>9\)) ⟹ major axis along \(x\)-axis, \(a=5,b=3\).
**Work** \(c=\sqrt{25-9}=4\). **Check/Result:** foci \((\pm4,0)\), vertices \((\pm5,0)\), major axis \(10\), minor axis \(6\), \(e=4/5\), latus rectum \(2b^2/a=18/5\).

#### 10.5 Solved — The "which axis is major" trap (NCERT Example 10)

**Given** \(9x^2+4y^2=36\). Divide by 36: \(\dfrac{x^2}{4}+\dfrac{y^2}{9}=1\).

> **Watch out:** \(x\) is written first, but that doesn't make it the major axis. Compare the **denominators**: \(9>4\), so the major axis is along \(y\), giving \(a=3\) (associated with \(y^2\)) and \(b=2\) (associated with \(x^2\)) — not the other way around.

\(c=\sqrt{9-4}=\sqrt5\), \(e=\sqrt5/3\). Foci \((0,\pm\sqrt5)\), vertices \((0,\pm3)\), major axis \(6\), minor axis \(4\).

#### 10.5 Solved — Building from vertices and foci (NCERT Example 11)

Vertices \((\pm13,0)\), foci \((\pm5,0)\) ⟹ \(a=13,c=5\), so \(b^2=169-25=144\). \(\boxed{\dfrac{x^2}{169}+\dfrac{y^2}{144}=1}\).

#### 10.5 Solved — Building from major-axis length and foci on the y-axis (NCERT Example 12)

Major axis length \(20 \Rightarrow a=10\); foci \((0,\pm5)\Rightarrow c=5\); \(b^2=100-25=75\). Foci on \(y\)-axis ⟹ form \(\dfrac{x^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{a^2}=1\): \(\boxed{\dfrac{x^2}{75}+\dfrac{y^2}{100}=1}\).

#### 10.5 Solved — Two points and a stated major axis (NCERT Example 13)

Major axis along \(x\), through \((4,3)\) and \((-1,4)\). Substituting both points into \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\) and writing \(u=1/a^2,\ v=1/b^2\):
\[
16u+9v=1,\qquad u+16v=1
\]
Solving simultaneously: \(v=\dfrac{15}{247}\), \(u=\dfrac{7}{247}\), so \(a^2=\dfrac{247}{7}\), \(b^2=\dfrac{247}{15}\).
**Check:** \(16\cdot\frac{7}{247}+9\cdot\frac{15}{247}=\frac{112+135}{247}=1\) ✓, and \(\frac{7}{247}+16\cdot\frac{15}{247}=\frac{7+240}{247}=1\) ✓.
\[
\boxed{7x^2+15y^2=247}
\]

## 10.6 Hyperbola ⭐⭐⭐

**Definition.** A hyperbola is the set of all points in a plane whose distances from two fixed points (the **foci**) differ by a constant — "difference" meaning farther-point distance minus nearer-point distance.

The midpoint of the foci is the **centre**; the line through the foci is the **transverse axis**; the perpendicular line through the centre is the **conjugate axis**; where the hyperbola meets the transverse axis are the **vertices**. Distance between foci \(=2c\); distance between vertices \(=2a\); \(b=\sqrt{c^2-a^2}\), with \(2b\) the length of the conjugate axis.

**Confirming the constant equals \(2a\).** Taking \(P\) at each vertex \(A\) and \(B\) in turn and using \(BF_1-BF_2=AF_2-AF_1\) (both equal the same defining constant) leads to \(AF_1=BF_2\), and then \(BF_1-BF_2 = BA = 2a\) — so the "constant difference" is exactly the transverse-axis length, matching the ellipse's analogous fact for the sum.

### 10.6.1 Eccentricity

\[
\boxed{e=\frac{c}{a}}, \qquad e \ge 1 \text{ always, since } c\ge a
\]
(contrast with the ellipse, where \(0<e<1\) — the boundary case \(e=1\) belongs to the parabola, not either of these).

### 10.6.2 Standard equation — derive one orientation, then use symmetry

**Set-up.** Foci \(F_1(-c,0)\), \(F_2(c,0)\); constant difference \(2a\), with \(PF_1-PF_2=2a\) for \(P\) to the right of \(x=a\). The same isolate-a-radical-then-square-twice method as the ellipse (safe here for the same reason — the quantity squared at each step is non-negative for a genuine point on that branch) reduces to
\[
\frac{cx}{a}-a=\sqrt{(x-c)^2+y^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1}\quad(b^2=c^2-a^2)
\]
Checking the converse (points to the *left* of \(x=-a\) satisfy \(PF_2-PF_1=2a\) instead, with the sign of the linear term in \(PF_1,PF_2\) flipping) confirms both branches of the curve are captured by the single equation \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\), not just the branch used to set it up.

> **Note:** a hyperbola with \(a=b\) is called **equilateral**.

**Finishing the other orientation.** As with the ellipse, swapping \(x\) and \(y\) (reflection in \(y=x\)) turns foci-on-\(x\)-axis into foci-on-\(y\)-axis with no fresh derivation: \(\dfrac{y^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{x^2}{b^2}=1\).

> **Watch out — the opposite rule from the ellipse:** for an ellipse, the *larger denominator* marks the major axis. For a hyperbola, there's no "larger denominator" test at all — the transverse axis is along whichever variable has the **positive** term. \(\dfrac{x^2}{9}-\dfrac{y^2}{16}=1\) has its transverse axis along \(x\) (length 6) even though \(16>9\); \(\dfrac{y^2}{25}-\dfrac{x^2}{16}=1\) has transverse axis along \(y\) (length 10). Mixing up the ellipse rule (compare sizes) with the hyperbola rule (compare signs) is one of the most common scoring errors in this chapter.

**Discussion.** From \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}=1+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}\ge1\), we get \(x\le-a\) or \(x\ge a\) — no part of the curve lies between the lines \(x=-a\) and \(x=a\); i.e. there is no real intercept on the conjugate axis.

### 10.6.3 Latus rectum

By the same substitute-the-focus-\(x\)-coordinate method used for the ellipse, the latus rectum of \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\) has length
\[
\boxed{\frac{2b^2}{a}}
\]

> **Watch out:** the *formula* \(2b^2/a\) is identical in shape for ellipse and hyperbola, but \(a\) means something different in each — semi-major axis for the ellipse, semi-transverse axis for the hyperbola. Don't assume a memorized number transfers between the two curves without re-checking which \(a\) you're using.

#### 10.6 Solved — Reading off every parameter (NCERT Example 14)

**(i)** \(\dfrac{x^2}{9}-\dfrac{y^2}{16}=1\): \(a=3,b=4,c=\sqrt{9+16}=5\). Foci \((\pm5,0)\), vertices \((\pm3,0)\), \(e=5/3\), latus rectum \(2(16)/3=32/3\).
**(ii)** \(y^2-16x^2=16\): divide by 16 → \(\dfrac{y^2}{16}-\dfrac{x^2}{1}=1\): \(a=4,b=1,c=\sqrt{17}\). Foci \((0,\pm\sqrt{17})\), vertices \((0,\pm4)\), \(e=\sqrt{17}/4\), latus rectum \(2(1)/4=1/2\).

#### 10.6 Solved — Building from foci and vertices (NCERT Example 15)

Foci \((0,\pm3)\), vertices \((0,\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2)\): \(a=\tfrac{\sqrt{11}}2\), \(c=3\), \(b^2=c^2-a^2=9-\tfrac{11}4=\tfrac{25}4\).
\[
\boxed{100y^2-44x^2=275}
\]
(clearing the fractions \(\tfrac{y^2}{11/4}-\tfrac{x^2}{25/4}=1\) by multiplying through by \(11\times25\)).

#### 10.6 Solved — Building from foci and latus rectum (NCERT Example 16)

Foci \((0,\pm12)\Rightarrow c=12\). Latus rectum \(36=2b^2/a \Rightarrow b^2=18a\). Then \(c^2=a^2+b^2\):
\[
144=a^2+18a \;\Longrightarrow\; a^2+18a-144=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; a=6 \text{ or } a=-24
\]
**Check:** \(a\) is a length, so discard the negative root: \(a=6\), \(b^2=108\).
\[
\boxed{3y^2-x^2=108}
\]

## Miscellaneous Worked Examples (applications) ⭐⭐

#### Solved — Parabolic mirror depth (NCERT Miscellaneous Example 17)

**Given** focus 5 cm from vertex, mirror 45 cm deep. **Approach** origin at vertex, axis along \(+x\): \(a=5\), so \(y^2=20x\).
**Work** at \(x=45\): \(y^2=900\Rightarrow y=\pm30\). **Result:** \(AB=2(30)=60\) cm.

#### Solved — Suspension-bridge cable (NCERT Miscellaneous Example 18)

**Given** supports 12 m apart, cable sags 3 cm at the centre, shape is a parabola. **Approach** put the vertex (lowest point of the cable) at the origin, axis vertical: \(x^2=4ay\). The supports sit 6 m either side of centre, 3 cm *above* the vertex, so the parabola passes through \((6,\ 3/100)\):
\[
36=4a\left(\frac{3}{100}\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; a=\frac{36\times100}{12}=300\text{ m}
\]
**Find:** how far from the centre is the *deflection* (sag below the support level) equal to 1 cm?

> **Filling a gap the source skips:** if the support level sits 3 cm above the vertex, then "1 cm of deflection" at some point means the cable there is only \(3-1=2\) cm above the vertex — that's why the next step uses \(y=2/100\) m, not \(1/100\) m.

\[
x^2=4(300)\left(\frac{2}{100}\right)=24 \;\Longrightarrow\; x=\sqrt{24}=2\sqrt6\ \text{m}
\]

#### Solved — Rod sliding between two axes traces an ellipse (NCERT Miscellaneous Example 19)

**Given** rod \(AB\), length 15 cm, end \(A\) on the \(x\)-axis, end \(B\) on the \(y\)-axis, and \(P(x,y)\) on the rod with \(AP=6\) cm (so \(PB=9\) cm).
**Approach** let \(\theta\) be the angle the rod makes with the \(x\)-axis; drop perpendiculars from \(P\) to both axes.
**Work** From the right triangle using \(PB=9\) as hypotenuse: \(\cos\theta = x/9\). From the right triangle using \(PA=6\) as hypotenuse: \(\sin\theta=y/6\). Since \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\):
\[
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{81}+\frac{y^2}{36}=1}
\]
**Check:** this is an ellipse with \(a=9,b=6\) — sensible, since \(a\) should relate to the longer segment \(PB=9\) and \(b\) to the shorter \(AP=6\). The locus of *any* point on a sliding rod like this is always an ellipse; this is the classic "trammel of Archimedes" construction.

## Points to Ponder ⭐⭐

> **Squaring can introduce extraneous solutions — but not here, and here's why.** Every derivation above squares both sides of a distance equation at least once. That's normally a red flag for extraneous roots (as in \(\sqrt{x+3}=x-3\)). It's safe in every derivation above only because each side being squared is a distance or an explicitly non-negative combination for the point actually being described — always re-check that condition before trusting a squared-through derivation elsewhere.

> **Ellipse rule vs. hyperbola rule — don't cross the wires.** For an ellipse, compare the *sizes* of the two denominators (bigger denominator ⟹ that axis is major). For a hyperbola, compare the *signs* of the two terms (positive term ⟹ that axis is transverse). These are different tests, and applying the ellipse test to a hyperbola (or vice versa) is a very common exam slip.

> **The latus-rectum formula \(2b^2/a\) looks identical for ellipse and hyperbola** — but double-check which curve's \(a\) and \(b\) you've just computed before reusing a number from one part of a problem in another part.

> **Domain discipline for the parabola:** \(y^2=4ax\) with \(a>0\) forces \(x\ge0\) — there is no real point on the curve with \(x<0\). If a problem's algebra produces a negative \(x\) "solution" for this form, it's extraneous and must be dropped, not rounded off as a numerical curiosity.

> **A unifying remark (beyond what this chapter derives):** eccentricity quietly orders the whole family — circle (\(e=0\)), ellipse (\(0<e<1\)), parabola (\(e=1\)), hyperbola (\(e>1\)). This chapter defines eccentricity only for the ellipse and hyperbola (via \(c/a\)) and does not derive a focus–directrix eccentricity definition for them the way it does for the parabola; connecting all four via a single focus–directrix ratio is explicitly flagged in the source as beyond this chapter's scope, so treat this row as a conceptual signpost for later study, not as a formula to quote in an answer here.

> **Reading a general second-degree-in-\(x,y\) equation:** before you can apply any formula above, first divide through so the equation is in "\(=1\)" (ellipse/hyperbola) or isolated-squared-term (parabola/circle) form — a coefficient sitting in front of \(x^2\) or \(y^2\) that hasn't been normalized to 1 is the single most common source of a wrong \(a\), \(b\), or radius (see Example 10 above).
