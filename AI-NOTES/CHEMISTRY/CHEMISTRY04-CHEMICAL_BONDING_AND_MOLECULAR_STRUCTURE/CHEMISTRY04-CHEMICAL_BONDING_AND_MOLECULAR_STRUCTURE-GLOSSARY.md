# Chemistry | Chapter 04 | Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure | GLOSSARY

## A

**Antibonding Molecular Orbital (σ\*, π\*)**
Formed by *subtracting* two atomic orbitals in LCAO (σ\* = ψ_A − ψ_B); higher in energy than either parent orbital, with a nodal plane of zero electron density between the nuclei, so electrons placed here destabilise the molecule — the opposite of a **bonding molecular orbital**. → NOTES §10.3. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Axial Bond**
In a trigonal bipyramidal molecule (e.g. PCl₅), either of the two bonds perpendicular to the equatorial plane, at 90° to all three equatorial bonds. Axial bonds are longer and weaker than **equatorial bonds**, because each axial pair faces 90° repulsion from three equatorial pairs rather than two. → NOTES §9.5. *(NEET/JEE)*

---

## B

**Bond Angle**
The angle between two bonding-electron-pair orbitals at a molecule's central atom, fixed by its VSEPR geometry and measured spectroscopically — e.g. 104.5° in H₂O, 109.5° in CH₄. → NOTES §5.2. *(Board/NEET)*

**Bond Enthalpy (Bond Dissociation Enthalpy)**
The energy needed to break one mole of a specific bond in the gaseous state (kJ mol⁻¹); a larger value means a stronger bond. Diatomic molecules have one exact value (H–H = 435.8 kJ mol⁻¹); polyatomic molecules instead use a **mean bond enthalpy**, since successive bonds of the same type break at slightly different energies. → NOTES §5.3. *(Board/NEET)*

**Bond Length**
The equilibrium internuclear distance between two bonded atoms — the separation at which potential energy is minimum — approximated for a covalent A–B bond as the sum of the two **covalent radii**. Bond length falls as bond order rises (triple < double < single). → NOTES §5.1. *(Board/NEET)*

**Bond Order**
By the Lewis description, the number of shared electron pairs between two atoms (1, 2, 3 for single/double/triple); by MO theory, ½(N_b − N_a). A positive bond order means a stable species, higher bond order tracks with shorter bond length and higher bond enthalpy, and resonance can give fractional values (1.33 in CO₃²⁻, 1.5 in O₃). → NOTES §5.4, §10.6. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Bonding Molecular Orbital (σ, π)**
Formed by *adding* two atomic orbitals in LCAO (σ = ψ_A + ψ_B); lower in energy than either parent orbital, with electron density concentrated between the nuclei, so electrons here stabilise the molecule — the opposite of an **antibonding molecular orbital**. → NOTES §10.3. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

## C

**Canonical Forms (Resonance Structures)**
The individual Lewis structures — identical nuclear positions and electron count, differing only in π-electron/lone-pair placement — whose weighted composite is a resonance-stabilised molecule's real structure. They have no independent existence; the molecule does not switch between them. → NOTES §5.5. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Chemical Bond**
The attractive force holding two or more atoms or ions together in a stable species, arising because bonded systems sit at lower energy than isolated atoms. This chapter develops two mechanisms in depth — **ionic bond** (electron transfer, §4) and **covalent bond** (electron sharing, §1–§2) — plus **hydrogen bond** as a weaker intermolecular case (§11). → NOTES §1–§2, §4, §11. *(Board)*

**Covalent Bond**
A bond formed by two atoms mutually sharing one or more electron pairs, each atom contributing at least one electron; the first shared pair is always a **sigma bond**, any further pairs are **pi bonds**. → NOTES §2.2. *(Board/NEET)*

**Covalent Radius**
Half the internuclear distance between two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond; used to estimate bond length as R = r_A + r_B. Always smaller than the same atom's **van der Waals radius**, since a bonded pair of atoms sits closer together than a non-bonded pair. → NOTES §5.1. *(Board)*

## D

**Debye (D)**
The unit of **dipole moment**: 1 D = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C m, named after Peter Debye (Nobel Prize, 1936). Most polar molecules fall in the 0–4 D range. → NOTES §6.2. *(Board/NEET)*

**Diamagnetic**
A species in which every molecular orbital is fully paired, so it is weakly repelled by a magnetic field — e.g. H₂, N₂, F₂. Contrast with **paramagnetic**, where one or more MOs hold an unpaired electron. → NOTES §10.6–§10.7. *(NEET/JEE)*

**Dipole Moment (µ)**
A vector measure of bond or molecular polarity, µ = Q × r (charge magnitude × separation of charge centres), in Debye. For a polyatomic molecule, the net µ is the vector sum of all bond dipoles, which can cancel to zero by symmetry even when individual bonds are polar (e.g. CO₂, CCl₄). → NOTES §6.2. *(Board/NEET)*

**dsp² Hybridisation**
Mixing of one inner (n−1)d orbital with one s and two p orbitals to give four hybrid orbitals in **square planar** geometry (90°), seen in transition-metal complexes such as [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻. Distinguished from **sp³ hybridisation** (tetrahedral, no d orbital) by both geometry and orbital composition. → NOTES §9.5. *(NEET/JEE)*

## E

**Electrovalent Bond (Ionic Bond)**
*See Ionic Bond.*

**Equatorial Bond**
In a trigonal bipyramidal molecule, any of the three bonds lying in the horizontal plane, 120° apart from each other. Equatorial bonds are shorter and stronger than **axial bonds**, since each equatorial pair faces less 90°-angle repulsion. → NOTES §9.5. *(NEET/JEE)*

**Expanded Octet**
A central atom surrounded by more than 8 electrons, possible only from the third period onward, where energetically accessible 3d orbitals allow extra bonding (e.g. PF₅: 10e⁻ at P; SF₆: 12e⁻ at S). Not automatic for every heavier p-block atom — SCl₂, for instance, still obeys a plain octet. → NOTES §3.3. *(Board/NEET)*

---

## F

**Fajans' Rules**
Kazimierz Fajans' (1923) conditions under which an otherwise-ionic bond gains covalent character, as the cation polarises the anion's electron cloud toward itself: a smaller, more highly charged cation and a larger anion both increase covalent character, as does a transition-metal-type (n−1)dⁿns⁰ cation over a same-size noble-gas-type cation. → NOTES §4.4. *(NEET/JEE)*

**Formal Charge**
The charge assigned to an atom in a Lewis structure under the simplifying assumption of *equal* electron sharing: F.C. = (valence e⁻ of the free atom) − (non-bonding e⁻) − ½(bonding e⁻). Not a real, measurable charge — used to pick the best Lewis structure, preferring the one with the smallest formal charges. → NOTES §2.4. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

---

## H

**Hybridisation**
The mixing of an atom's near-equal-energy valence orbitals into a new set of equivalent hybrid orbitals, proposed by Pauling to explain observed molecular geometries (e.g. tetrahedral CH₄) that unhybridised orbitals alone cannot. The hybridisation type always matches the VSEPR-predicted electron-domain count — sp/sp²/sp³/sp³d/sp³d² for 2/3/4/5/6 domains. → NOTES §9.1. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Hydrogen Bond**
A weak electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to F, O, or N and another highly electronegative F/O/N atom nearby; weaker than a covalent bond, stronger than van der Waals forces, drawn as a dotted line. Occurs as **intermolecular** (between separate molecules) or **intramolecular** (within one molecule) hydrogen bonding. → NOTES §11.1. *(Board/NEET)*

---

## I

**Incomplete Octet**
A central atom left with fewer than 8 electrons after bonding, occurring for elements with fewer than 4 valence electrons (e.g. B in BF₃: 6e⁻ around B). These electron-deficient species commonly act as **Lewis acids** (electron-pair acceptors). → NOTES §3.1. *(Board/NEET)*

**Intermolecular Hydrogen Bond**
A hydrogen bond between two separate molecules (e.g. the H···F chains in HF). Raises melting point, boiling point, viscosity, and surface tension — contrast with an **intramolecular hydrogen bond**, which instead removes a molecule from this network. → NOTES §11.3. *(Board/NEET)*

**Intramolecular Hydrogen Bond**
A hydrogen bond formed within a single molecule, closing a 5- or 6-membered ring (e.g. o-nitrophenol). Because it makes the molecule effectively cyclic, it lowers boiling/melting point relative to an isomer that can only form **intermolecular hydrogen bonds** (e.g. o- vs p-nitrophenol). → NOTES §11.3. *(Board/NEET)*

**Ionic Bond (Electrovalent Bond)**
A bond formed by complete electron transfer — typically a low-ionization-enthalpy metal to a non-metal with strongly negative electron gain enthalpy — producing ions held together by electrostatic (coulombic) attraction. Stability is governed by **lattice enthalpy**, not merely whether each ion individually reached a noble-gas configuration. → NOTES §4.1–§4.2. *(Board/NEET)*

---

## K

**Kernel**
In Lewis's original atomic model, the positively charged core of an atom — nucleus plus all non-valence (inner) electrons — surrounded by the reactive valence shell. → NOTES §1.1. *(Board)*

**Kössel-Lewis Approach**
The first electronic theory of bonding (1916): Kössel explained ionic bonding as electron transfer toward a noble-gas configuration, while Lewis explained covalent bonding as electron-pair sharing, introducing Lewis symbols and dot structures. Both rest on the **octet rule**. → NOTES §1. *(Board/NEET)*

---

## L

**Lattice Enthalpy**
The energy required to completely separate one mole of a crystalline ionic solid into its gaseous ions (always positive; 788 kJ mol⁻¹ for NaCl). Cannot be measured directly from simple pairwise ionic attraction, because the crystal is three-dimensional — a higher lattice enthalpy means a more stable ionic compound. → NOTES §4.3. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**LCAO (Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals)**
The method by which MO theory builds a molecular orbital as ψ(MO) = ψ_A ± ψ_B: addition gives a **bonding molecular orbital**, subtraction gives an **antibonding molecular orbital**. Requires the combining orbitals to have comparable energy, matching symmetry about the molecular axis, and maximum overlap. → NOTES §10.3. *(NEET/JEE)*

**Lewis Acid/Base**
In the Lewis sense, an acid is an electron-pair *acceptor* (e.g. BF₃, AlCl₃, H⁺) and a base is an electron-pair *donor* (e.g. NH₃, H₂O); the electron-deficient species from this chapter's incomplete-octet compounds are the acids. → NOTES §3.1. *(NEET/JEE)*

**Lewis Dot Structure (Lewis Structure)**
A structural formula showing bonding electron pairs as lines and lone pairs as dots, built by counting valence electrons, placing the least-electronegative atom centrally, and distributing electrons to complete every atom's octet. Shows connectivity and electron count only — not the molecule's actual 3-D shape (that needs VSEPR or VB/MO theory). → NOTES §2.3. *(Board/NEET)*

**Lewis Symbols**
An element's valence electrons shown as dots around its symbol; the number of *unpaired* dots gives the element's covalence (bonding capacity), and the total dot count gives its group valence (or 8 minus the dot count, for elements past mid-period). → NOTES §1.1. *(Board)*

**Lone Pair**
A pair of valence electrons on an atom that is not shared with another atom. Being localised on one atom rather than spread between two, a lone pair occupies more space and repels other electron pairs more strongly than a **bond pair** does, per VSEPR. → NOTES §7.2–§7.3. *(Board/NEET)*

## M

**Mean (Average) Bond Enthalpy**
For a polyatomic molecule, the average of the successive bond dissociation enthalpies of one bond type, since each bond in the sequence breaks at a slightly different energy as the local chemical environment changes (e.g. water's mean O–H enthalpy = (502+427)/2 = 464.5 kJ mol⁻¹). → NOTES §5.3. *(Board/NEET)*

**Molecular Orbital (MO)**
An orbital extending over an entire molecule (polycentric, unlike an atomic orbital's single-nucleus, monocentric character), formed by LCAO and filled by the same Aufbau/Pauli/Hund rules as atomic orbitals, each holding at most 2 electrons. → NOTES §10.1–§10.4. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Molecular Orbital Theory (MO Theory)**
Hund and Mulliken's (1932) theory describing a molecule's electrons as occupying **molecular orbitals** delocalised over the whole molecule, built from LCAO. Unlike **VB theory**, it correctly predicts O₂'s paramagnetism, directly from Hund's-rule filling of its degenerate π* orbitals. → NOTES §10. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

---

## N

**Non-polar Covalent Bond**
A covalent bond between identical atoms, or atoms of equal electronegativity, where the shared pair sits exactly at the midpoint and the bond dipole is zero (e.g. H–H, Cl–Cl). Contrast with a **polar covalent bond**. → NOTES §6.1. *(Board)*

---

## O

**Octet Rule**
Kössel and Lewis's (1916) rule that atoms gain, lose, or share electrons to reach 8 valence electrons — a noble-gas configuration (2, for H and He). Fails for three families of species: **incomplete octet**, **odd-electron molecules**, and **expanded octet**. → NOTES §2.1, §3. *(Board/NEET)*

**Orbital Overlap**
The partial interpenetration of two atomic orbitals as they approach to bond. Same-phase (positive) overlap increases electron density between the nuclei and lowers energy (bonding); opposite-phase overlap is antibonding; perpendicular orientation gives zero overlap. Head-on overlap gives a **sigma bond**; sideways overlap gives a **pi bond**. → NOTES §8.3. *(NEET/JEE)*

---

## P

**Paramagnetic**
A species with one or more unpaired electrons in its molecular orbitals, so it is drawn into a magnetic field — most notably O₂, whose two degenerate π*2p orbitals each hold one unpaired electron. Contrast with **diamagnetic**. → NOTES §10.6–§10.7. *(NEET/JEE)*

**Pi (π) Bond**
A covalent bond formed by the sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, perpendicular to the internuclear axis, giving electron density in two lobes above and below the bond plane. Weaker than a **sigma bond** (less orbital overlap), always accompanies an existing σ bond in a multiple bond, and restricts rotation about the bond axis. → NOTES §8.4. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Polar Covalent Bond**
A covalent bond between atoms of different electronegativity, where the shared pair shifts toward the more electronegative atom, leaving δ+/δ− partial charges and a non-zero bond dipole; polarity increases with the electronegativity gap. Contrast with a **non-polar covalent bond**. → NOTES §6.1. *(Board/NEET)*

---

## R

**Resonance**
A description used when one Lewis structure cannot represent a molecule accurately: several equivalent **canonical forms** are drawn, and the real molecule is their weighted-average **resonance hybrid**, lower in energy than any single form. The molecule does *not* switch between canonical forms — it has one real, hybrid structure, unlike genuinely interconverting **tautomers**. → NOTES §5.5. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Resonance Hybrid**
The real, single structure of a resonance-stabilised molecule — the weighted average of all its canonical forms, with bond parameters intermediate between them (e.g. O₃'s two O–O bonds are both 128 pm, between single-bond 148 pm and double-bond 121 pm) and lower energy than any one form (the difference is the resonance energy). → NOTES §5.5. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

---

## S

**Sigma (σ) Bond**
A covalent bond formed by head-on overlap of two orbitals along the internuclear axis (s–s, s–p, or p–p), giving cylindrically symmetric electron density and allowing free rotation. Stronger than a **pi bond**, and always the first bond formed between any two atoms. → NOTES §8.4. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**sp Hybridisation**
Mixing of one s and one p orbital into two equivalent sp orbitals at 180° (50% s-character each), leaving two unhybridised p orbitals free for π bonding — e.g. BeCl₂ (no π bonds), C₂H₂ (2 π bonds). Also called diagonal hybridisation. → NOTES §9.2. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**sp² Hybridisation**
Mixing of one s and two p orbitals into three equivalent sp² orbitals at 120° in a plane (33% s-character each), leaving one unhybridised p orbital free for one π bond — e.g. BCl₃ (no π bond), C₂H₄ (one C=C π bond). → NOTES §9.3. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**sp³ Hybridisation**
Mixing of one s and three p orbitals into four equivalent sp³ orbitals toward the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart (25% s-character each), with no unhybridised p orbitals left — e.g. CH₄ (4 bond pairs, tetrahedral), NH₃ (3 bond + 1 lone pair, pyramidal), H₂O (2 bond + 2 lone pairs, bent). → NOTES §9.4. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**sp³d Hybridisation**
Mixing of one s, three p, and one d orbital into five hybrid orbitals in trigonal bipyramidal geometry — three **equatorial bonds** at 120°, two **axial bonds** at 90° to the equatorial plane — e.g. PCl₅, PF₅. → NOTES §9.5. *(NEET/JEE)*

**sp³d² Hybridisation**
Mixing of one s, three p, and two d orbitals into six hybrid orbitals in regular octahedral geometry, all angles 90° — e.g. SF₆. Distinct from **d²sp³ hybridisation**, which uses inner (n−1)d orbitals in transition-metal complexes rather than same-shell d orbitals. → NOTES §9.5. *(NEET/JEE)*

---

## V

**Valence Bond (VB) Theory**
Heitler and London's (1927) quantum-mechanical theory, developed further by Pauling: a covalent bond forms when two atomic orbitals overlap and their electrons pair with opposite spins. Explains bond energetics, directionality, and geometry via hybridisation — but fails to predict O₂'s paramagnetism, which **MO theory** gets right. → NOTES §8. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*

**Van der Waals Radius**
Half the internuclear distance between two adjacent, non-bonded atoms of the same element — always larger than that atom's **covalent radius**, since no bond pulls the atoms as close together. → NOTES §5.1. *(Board)*

**VSEPR Theory (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion)**
Sidgwick and Powell's (1940) model, refined by Nyholm and Gillespie (1957), for predicting molecular geometry: valence-shell electron pairs repel and arrange to minimise that repulsion, in the order lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair (a multiple bond counts as one pair for this purpose). Predicts shape only — it does not explain the underlying energetics. → NOTES §7. *(Board/NEET/JEE)*
