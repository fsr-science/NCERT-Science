# Chemistry | Chapter 03 | Classification of Elements and Periodicity | GLOSSARY

### Classification of Elements and Periodicity in Properties

---

> All terms arranged **alphabetically**. Each entry is a fast, standalone lookup — for the reasoning, worked examples, and data behind a term, follow its `§` pointer into NOTES.

---

## A

**Actinoids (Actinides)**
The **5f inner-transition series** of 14 radioactive metals from thorium (Th, Z=90) to lawrencium (Lr, Z=103), placed in **Period 7**; general configuration (n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴(n−1)d⁰⁻¹ns². Elements beyond uranium (Z>92) are the transuranium elements. §3.6.4

**Amphoteric Oxide**
An oxide that reacts with **both acids and bases** (e.g. Al₂O₃, ZnO), typically formed by elements near the centre of a period. Contrasts with a basic oxide (reacts only with acids) and an acidic oxide (reacts only with bases). §3.7.3

**Atomic Radius**
The effective size of an atom, taken as half the internuclear distance in a specific bonding context: **covalent radius** (non-metals, single-bonded), **metallic radius** (metals, in a crystal), or **van der Waals radius** (non-bonded neighbours, the largest of the three). Unqualified "atomic radius" means covalent or metallic radius, whichever applies to the element. §3.7.1(a)

---

## B

**Basic Oxide**
An oxide that reacts with water to form a base, or with an acid to form salt and water; formed by elements at the far left of a period (alkali and alkaline earth metals). Basicity increases down Groups 1 and 2 as metallic character increases. §3.7.3

---

## C

**Chalcogens**
The **Group 16** elements (O, S, Se, Te, Po), configuration ns²np⁴, which readily gain two electrons to reach a noble-gas configuration. §3.6.2

**Covalent Radius**
Half the internuclear distance between two identical atoms joined by a single covalent bond; used for non-metals, and the smallest of the three atomic-radius types for a given element (see Atomic Radius). §3.7.1(a)

---

## D

**d-Block Elements (Transition Elements)**
Elements of **Groups 3–12** in which the last electron enters the (n−1)d subshell, configuration (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns⁰⁻²; all are metals, typically forming coloured ions, multiple oxidation states, and acting as catalysts. Zn, Cd, and Hg have a completely filled d subshell and so lack most of these transition-metal properties. §3.6.3

**Diagonal Relationship**
The chemical similarity between a Period 2 element and the element diagonally below-right in the next group (Li–Mg, Be–Al), arising because the two have similar charge-to-radius ratios. §3.7.2(b)

**Dobereiner's Triads**
An 1829 classification by Johann Döbereiner grouping elements in threes where the middle element's atomic weight is approximately the mean of the other two (e.g. Li, Na, K); worked for only a handful of such triads. §3.2.1

---

## E

**Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)**
The net positive charge felt by a valence electron after subtracting the shielding of inner electrons: Zeff = Z − σ, always less than the true nuclear charge Z. A higher Zeff pulls the valence shell in tighter, producing a smaller radius, higher ionization enthalpy, and higher electronegativity. §3.5.1

**Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔegH)**
The enthalpy change when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom, X(g)+e⁻→X⁻(g); negative (exothermic) for most non-metals, positive (endothermic) for noble gases. Becomes more negative across a period and less negative down a group; chlorine's value is the most negative overall, not fluorine's, because fluorine's small n=2 shell crowds the incoming electron. §3.7.1(d)

**Electronegativity**
A comparative, not directly measurable, tendency of an atom to attract shared bonding electrons toward itself within a compound; the Pauling scale is most widely used, with fluorine fixed at 4.0. Increases across a period and decreases down a group, tracking the ionization-enthalpy trend — but it is not the same quantity as electron gain enthalpy: fluorine is the most electronegative element, yet chlorine's electron gain enthalpy is more negative. §3.7.1(e)

---

## F

**f-Block Elements (Inner-Transition Elements)**
Elements in which the last electron enters the (n−2)f subshell: the 4f **lanthanoids** (Ce–Lu, Period 6) and 5f **actinoids** (Th–Lr, Period 7), general configuration (n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴(n−1)d⁰⁻¹ns². Drawn as a separate panel below the main table purely to keep it compact; chemically they sit between Group 2 and Group 3. §3.6.4

---

## G

**Group (Family)**
A vertical column of the periodic table; elements in the same group share the same valence-shell configuration and so similar chemistry. IUPAC numbers groups 1–18, with Groups 1–2 as s-block, 3–12 as d-block, and 13–18 as p-block. §3.3.3

---

## H

**Halogens**
The **Group 17** elements (F, Cl, Br, I, At), configuration ns²np⁵, one electron short of a noble-gas configuration; they have the most negative electron gain enthalpies of any group and form −1 ions. §3.6.2

---

## I

**Inert Pair Effect**
The tendency of the outermost ns² electron pair to resist bonding in heavier p-block elements, so the element favours an oxidation state two below its group valence (e.g. Tl⁺ rather than Tl³⁺, Pb²⁺ rather than Pb⁴⁺). Becomes more pronounced down a p-block group.

**Ionic Radius**
The effective radius of an ion in a crystal: a **cation** is smaller than its parent atom (same nuclear charge pulling fewer electrons), an **anion** is larger (more electron–electron repulsion). Among isoelectronic species, radius shrinks as nuclear charge rises. §3.7.1(b)

**Ionization Enthalpy (Ionization Energy, ΔᵢH)**
The energy required to remove the outermost electron from an isolated, ground-state gaseous atom (or, for successive removals, from the resulting ion); always positive, and each successive value is larger than the last (ΔᵢH₁ < ΔᵢH₂ < …). Generally increases across a period and decreases down a group, with exceptions at B<Be and O<N from orbital-penetration and electron-pairing effects. §3.7.1(c)

**Isoelectronic Species**
Atoms or ions with the same number of electrons (e.g. O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, all with 10 electrons). Among isoelectronic species, radius depends only on nuclear charge: the higher the charge, the smaller the ion. §3.7.1(b)

---

## L

**Lanthanoids (Lanthanides)**
The **4f inner-transition series** of 14 elements from cerium (Ce, Z=58) to lutetium (Lu, Z=71), placed in **Period 6**; properties are very similar within the series. Undergo lanthanoid contraction — a steady decrease in radius across the series from poor 4f shielding — which makes the 5d elements unusually close in radius to their 4d counterparts. §3.6.4

**Law of Octaves (Newlands, 1865)**
John Newlands' 1865 observation that, arranged by increasing atomic weight, every eighth element resembled the first (like musical octaves); it held only up to calcium and broke down for heavier elements. §3.2.3

---

## M

**Mendeleev's Periodic Law**
Dmitri Mendeleev's 1869 statement that the properties of elements are a periodic function of their **atomic weight**; he arranged elements so similar-property elements fell in the same group, even reordering a few pairs (e.g. Te before I) against strict weight order. Superseded by the Modern Periodic Law, which reorders by atomic number instead. §3.2.4

**Metallic Radius**
Half the internuclear distance between adjacent atoms in a metallic crystal; larger than the covalent radius of the same element, since metallic bonding is less directional and packs atoms more loosely. §3.7.1(a)

**Metalloids (Semi-metals)**
Elements with properties intermediate between metals and non-metals, lying along the **zig-zag border** of the periodic table (Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te); typically semiconductors, with metallic lustre but non-metal-like brittleness. §3.6.5

**Modern Periodic Law**
The 1913 restatement, following Moseley's work, that the properties of elements are periodic functions of their **atomic number** rather than atomic weight — resolving anomalies (e.g. Ar placed before K) that Mendeleev's atomic-weight version could not justify. §3.3.2

**Moseley's Experiment (1913)**
Henry Moseley's finding that plotting √ν (X-ray emission frequency) against atomic number Z gives a straight line, while plotting against atomic weight does not — establishing atomic number as the more fundamental ordering property and prompting the Modern Periodic Law. §3.3.1

---

## N

**Newlands' Law of Octaves**
*See Law of Octaves.*

**Noble Gases (Group 18)**
Elements He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn with a completely filled valence shell (ns²np⁶, except He at 1s²), giving very low reactivity, the highest ionization enthalpy in each period, and a positive electron gain enthalpy. Their tabulated radii are van der Waals radii, not on the same scale as a neighbouring halogen's covalent radius. §3.6.2

---

## O

**Oxidation State (Oxidation Number)**
The formal charge assigned to an atom in a compound on the assumption that every bond is fully ionic, with shared electrons given entirely to the more electronegative atom — distinct from valence, a simpler electron-counting rule that ignores which atom is more electronegative. Also distinct from covalency (the number of bonds formed): e.g. Al in [AlCl(H₂O)₅]²⁺ has oxidation state +3 but covalency 6. §3.7.2(a)

---

## P

**p-Block Elements**
Elements of **Groups 13–18** in which the last electron enters the outermost p-subshell, configuration ns²np¹ to ns²np⁶; together with the s-block, called Representative (Main-group) Elements. Includes most non-metals and metalloids alongside some metals (Al, Ga, Sn, Pb, Bi, Tl); each period ends in a noble gas. §3.6.2

**Pauling Scale**
The most widely used electronegativity scale, proposed by Linus Pauling from thermochemical bond-energy data; fluorine is fixed at the reference maximum of 4.0. §3.7.1(e)

**Period**
A horizontal row of the periodic table; the period number equals the highest principal quantum number (n) occupied by that row's elements. There are 7 periods in total. §3.3.3

**Periodic Law**
*See Mendeleev's Periodic Law or Modern Periodic Law.*

---

## R

**Representative Elements (Main-Group Elements)**
The s-block (Groups 1–2) and p-block (Groups 13–18) elements together, whose valence electrons occupy only s or p subshells; contrasted with the d-block (transition) and f-block (inner-transition) elements. §3.6.2

---

## S

**s-Block Elements**
Elements of **Groups 1** (alkali metals, ns¹) and **2** (alkaline earth metals, ns²); reactive metals with low ionization enthalpy, never found free in nature, forming predominantly ionic compounds except Li and Be. §3.6.1

**Shielding Effect (Screening Effect)**
The reduction in nuclear charge felt by an outer electron, caused by inner (core) electrons repelling it away from the nucleus; s-electrons shield more effectively than p, d, or f electrons of the same shell. Greater shielding lowers effective nuclear charge, which increases atomic radius and lowers ionization enthalpy. §3.5.1

---

## T

**Transuranium Elements**
All elements with atomic number greater than 92 (uranium); produced artificially, radioactive with short half-lives, and — for the heaviest — synthesised only a few atoms at a time. §3.6.4

**Triads**
*See Dobereiner's Triads.*

---

## V

**Valence**
The combining capacity of an element — for a representative element, its number of valence electrons or 8 minus that number, whichever applies to its group. Distinct from oxidation state (which additionally depends on relative electronegativity in a specific bond); now often replaced by oxidation state for transition metals. §3.7.2(a)

**van der Waals Radius**
Half the distance between the nuclei of two adjacent, non-bonded atoms (as in a solid noble gas); the largest of the three atomic-radius types for a given element, since no bond pulls the atoms together (see Atomic Radius). §3.7.1(a)

---

## Z

**Zeff (Effective Nuclear Charge)**
*See Effective Nuclear Charge.*

**Zig-Zag Line (in the Periodic Table)**
The diagonal line separating metals (left) from non-metals (right) in the periodic table; the elements bordering it (Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) are the metalloids. §3.6.5
