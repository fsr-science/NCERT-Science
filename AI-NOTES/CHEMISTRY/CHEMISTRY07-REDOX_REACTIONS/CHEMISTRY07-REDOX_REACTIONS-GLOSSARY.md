# Chemistry | Chapter 07 | Redox Reactions | GLOSSARY

> All terms arranged **alphabetically**. Each entry is a standalone, unambiguous answer to "what is this" — for the reasoning behind a rule or a worked derivation, follow the § pointer into NOTES.

---

## A

**Activity Series (Metal Activity Series)**
A list of metals in decreasing order of their tendency to lose electrons, built from direct metal-displacement experiments (§7.2.2): a metal higher in the list displaces the ion of any metal below it from solution. It is not the same list as the Electrochemical Series — that ranks half-reactions of *any* species (not just metals) by quantitative standard electrode potential; the two agree closely for common metals but are built differently. §7.2.2

**Anode**
The electrode in a galvanic cell where oxidation occurs and electrons flow out into the external circuit, making it the **negative** electrode (mnemonic: **"AN OX" — ANode: OXidation**). §7.4.1

---

## C

**Cathode**
The electrode in a galvanic cell where reduction occurs and electrons flow in from the external circuit, making it the **positive** electrode (mnemonic: **"RED CAT" — REDuction at CAThode**). §7.4.1

**Combination Redox Reaction**
A redox reaction of the shape A + B → C. Commonly at least one of A or B is elemental (as in all combustion reactions using O₂), but this is a shortcut, not the rule — the reaction is redox only if an oxidation number actually changes, and a combination reaction with no elemental reactant can still be redox. §7.3.5

**Competitive Electron Transfer**
The tendency of one metal to donate electrons to another metal's ion in solution, directly analogous to competition for protons among acids; comparing enough of these reactions is how the Activity Series is built. §7.2.2

---

## D

**Daniell Cell**
A galvanic cell built from the reaction Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), with the two half-reactions physically separated (Zn in ZnSO₄(aq), Cu in CuSO₄(aq), joined by a salt bridge and an external wire) so the electron transfer runs through the circuit as usable current instead of as direct atom-to-atom heat. §7.4.1

**Decomposition Redox Reaction**
A redox reaction in which a compound breaks into two or more simpler substances, commonly with at least one elemental product; as with combination reactions, the true test is a change in oxidation number, not the elemental-product shape alone (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ has that shape but is not redox). §7.3.5

**Disproportionation Reaction**
A redox reaction in which one element, starting from a single oxidation state, ends up simultaneously oxidised and reduced in the products. Requires the starting oxidation state to be **intermediate** among at least three oxidation states accessible to that element — this rules out F₂ (never positive) and ClO₄⁻ (Cl already at its ceiling of +7), both of which cannot disproportionate. §7.3.5, §7.3.4

**Displacement Redox Reaction**
A redox reaction in which one element replaces another in a compound (X + YZ → XZ + Y). Metal displacement follows the Activity Series; non-metal displacement covers hydrogen (reactive metals displacing H from water or acid) and halogens (F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ in oxidising power — a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide, except F₂ itself, which reacts with the water before it can displace anything). §7.3.5

---

## E

**Electrochemical Series**
A table of half-reactions (reduction reactions) ordered by decreasing standard electrode potential \(E^\circ\): the species at the top are the strongest oxidising agents, the species at the bottom the strongest reducing agents. It is the quantitative generalisation of the Activity Series to any redox couple, not just metals, and is the tool used to predict feasibility (§7.4.3's feasibility rule). §7.4.3

**Electrode**
A conductor, usually a metal rod, in contact with an electrolyte solution, through which current enters or leaves a cell; a galvanic cell has two — the anode and the cathode. §7.4.1

**Electrode Potential**
The tendency of the species at an electrode to remain in its oxidised or reduced form. It cannot be measured for a single electrode in isolation — every value is measured relative to the Standard Hydrogen Electrode. §7.4.2

**External-Indicator Titration**
A redox titration in which the titrant itself gives no colour change at the endpoint, so a separate indicator substance is added to signal it — e.g. diphenylamine turning intense blue just after equivalence in a Cr₂O₇²⁻ titration. Distinguish from a self-indicating titration (below), where the titrant's own colour marks the endpoint. §7.3.7

---

## F

**Fractional Oxidation Number**
An oxidation number that comes out non-integer when the algebraic-sum rule is averaged over every atom of an element in a species — usually because the real molecule holds that element in more than one whole-number state at different positions (C₃O₂: real states +2, 0, +2, average +4/3), though O₂⁺ (+½) and O₂⁻ (–½) are genuinely fractional rather than averages. Never treat a fractional value as the literal state of every atom without checking the structure. §7.3.4

---

## G

**Galvanic Cell (Voltaic Cell)**
An electrochemical cell in which a spontaneous redox reaction generates electrical energy, via two physically separated half-reactions (anode: oxidation; cathode: reduction) connected by an external circuit and a salt bridge. The Daniell cell is the standard example in this chapter. §7.4.1

---

## H

**Half-Reaction**
One of the two halves an overall redox reaction splits into, each written to show electron transfer explicitly — an oxidation half shows electrons as a product, a reduction half shows them as a reactant. Adding the two halves and cancelling the electrons recovers the overall balanced equation. §7.2.1

**Half-Reaction Method (Ion-Electron Method)**
A redox-balancing method that splits the equation into oxidation and reduction half-reactions, balances each separately (atoms, then O with H₂O, then H with H⁺ — in basic medium followed by one OH⁻ per H⁺ added to both sides — then charge with electrons), and adds them after equalising electron counts. Distinguish from the Oxidation Number Method, which tracks total O.N. change instead of splitting into explicit half-reactions. §7.3.6

**Hydrogen Displacement Reaction**
A non-metal displacement reaction in which a metal displaces hydrogen from water or an acid. Reactivity divides the common lab metals into three bands: very active metals (Na, K, Ca) react with cold water, less active metals (Mg, Fe) need steam, and many metals (Zn, Fe among them) displace H from acids; Ag and Au do neither. §7.3.5

---

## I

**Iodometric Titration**
An indirect redox titration in which the analyte first liberates I₂ from excess I⁻ (e.g. \(2\text{Cu}^{2+} + 4\text{I}^- \to \text{Cu}_2\text{I}_2 + \text{I}_2\)), and the liberated I₂ is then titrated against standard thiosulphate with starch as the indicator — the deep blue starch–iodine colour disappears sharply at the endpoint. §7.3.7

---

## L

**Layer Test**
A lab test for Br⁻ or I⁻: chlorine water displaces the halogen, which is then extracted into a CCl₄ layer for identification by colour (orange-brown for Br₂, violet for I₂). §7.3.5

---

## O

**Oxidation (Classical)**
Addition of oxygen or another electronegative element to a substance, or removal of hydrogen or an electropositive element from it — the original, narrowest of this chapter's three definitions of oxidation. Distinguish from Oxidation (Electronic) and Oxidation (Oxidation-Number), the two definitions that generalise it. §7.1.1

**Oxidation (Electronic)**
Loss of one or more electrons by a species (mnemonic: **OIL**, Oxidation Is Loss), making that species the reducing agent. Distinguish from Oxidation (Classical), the narrower O/H-based definition this one generalises. §7.2.1

**Oxidation (Oxidation-Number)**
An increase in a species' oxidation number — the definition general enough to apply even where electron transfer is only partial, as in covalent bonds. §7.3.3

**Oxidation Number (Oxidation State)**
The hypothetical charge an atom would carry if every bond to it were treated as fully ionic, with the shared electron pair assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom (§7.3's six rules give the procedure). It is a book-keeping device, not a real physical charge, and should never be confused with formal charge or the literal charge on an atom — the two coincide only for genuinely ionic monoatomic species. §7.3

**Oxidation Number Method**
A redox-balancing method based on the principle that total increase in oxidation number equals total decrease (electron conservation): assign O.N. to every atom, scale coefficients so the increases and decreases match, then balance charge with H⁺/OH⁻ and finish with H₂O for hydrogen. Distinguish from the Half-Reaction Method, which splits the equation into explicit half-reactions instead of tracking O.N. change directly. §7.3.6

**Oxidising Agent (Oxidant)**
The species that accepts electrons from another species, causing it to be oxidised, while the oxidising agent itself is reduced (its own O.N. decreases). In a galvanic cell, the oxidising agent is reduced at the cathode. §7.2.1

---

## R

**Redox Couple**
The oxidised and reduced forms of a species written together as Oxidised/Reduced (e.g. Zn²⁺/Zn, Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺); its standard electrode potential measures the couple's tendency to stay oxidised or reduced. §7.4.1

**Redox Reaction**
A reaction in which one species is oxidised and another is simultaneously reduced — the portmanteau "redox" names this pairing, which §7.1.3 shows never happens as a lone oxidation or lone reduction. Electrons lost by the reducing agent equal electrons gained by the oxidising agent. §7.1.3

**Redox Titration**
A volumetric method that uses a redox reaction between the analyte and a standard solution to find the analyte's concentration; how the endpoint is detected is what distinguishes the three variants used in this chapter — see Self-Indicator, External-Indicator Titration, and Iodometric Titration. §7.3.7

**Reducing Agent (Reductant)**
The species that donates electrons to another species, causing it to be reduced, while the reducing agent itself is oxidised (its own O.N. increases). In a galvanic cell, the reducing agent is oxidised at the anode. §7.2.1

**Reduction (Classical)**
Removal of oxygen or another electronegative element from a substance, or addition of hydrogen or an electropositive element to it — the classical counterpart to Oxidation (Classical), broadened the same way. §7.1.2

**Reduction (Electronic)**
Gain of one or more electrons by a species (mnemonic: **RIG**, Reduction Is Gain), making that species the oxidising agent. Distinguish from Reduction (Classical), the narrower O/H-based definition this one generalises. §7.2.1

**Reduction (Oxidation-Number)**
A decrease in a species' oxidation number — the oxidation-number counterpart to Oxidation (Oxidation-Number). §7.3.3

---

## S

**Salt Bridge**
A tube of concentrated electrolyte (commonly KCl in agar) connecting the two half-cells of a galvanic cell without letting the solutions mix; ion migration through it completes the internal circuit and keeps both solutions electrically neutral as the reaction proceeds. Without it, charge would build up and current would stop. §7.4.1

**Self-Indicator (Self-Indicating Titration)**
A redox titration in which the titrant is deeply coloured enough to signal its own endpoint, with no separate indicator needed — MnO₄⁻ is the standard example, going from intense purple to a lasting faint pink once the last reductant is consumed, detectable down to about \(10^{-6}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\). Distinguish from an External-Indicator Titration, which needs an added substance to show the endpoint. §7.3.7

**Standard Electrode Potential (E°)**
The electrode potential of a couple measured under standard conditions (1 M for every dissolved species, 1 atm for any gas, 298 K), tabulated as a reduction potential against the Standard Hydrogen Electrode. A more positive \(E^\circ\) means a stronger oxidising agent (oxidised form); a more negative \(E^\circ\) means a stronger reducing agent (reduced form) — the sign describes the couple relative to H⁺/H₂, not "strength" in any absolute sense. §7.4.2, §7.4.3

**Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)**
The universal reference electrode for standard electrode potentials, assigned \(E^\circ = 0.00\ \text{V}\) by convention: a platinum wire in 1 M H⁺(aq) with H₂ gas at 1 atm bubbled over it, realising \(2\text{H}^+(aq) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{(g)}\). Every other value in the Electrochemical Series is measured against it. §7.4.2

**Stock Notation**
Alfred Stock's convention of writing a metal's oxidation number as a Roman numeral in parentheses after its symbol, used to distinguish compounds of a metal that shows more than one oxidation state (e.g. Fe(II)Cl₂ vs Fe(III)Cl₃). The Hg(I) case is a common trap: it names the dimeric ion Hg₂²⁺, written Hg₂Cl₂, never a monomeric "HgCl". §7.3.2
